\section{Temporal reconstruction}
\begin{frame}{Events fix each pixel's brightness increments and leave one constant free}
\begin{columns}[c]
\column{.50\textwidth}
{\small\color{muted}Observation at one pixel}\par
Choose a unit-mass temporal kernel $\phi_{s_k}$:
\[e(t)=\sum_{k\text{ at this pixel}}p_k\phi_{s_k}(t),\]
\[y_i=\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}e(t)\,dt.\]
\column{.46\textwidth}
{\small\color{muted}Increments of the unknown history}\par
\[\A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&&\\&-1&1&\\&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},\]
\[\A\mathbf f\approx\mathbf y,\qquad\ker\A=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf1\}.\]
\end{columns}

Positive definiteness of the integrated matrix controls the mean: square $\norm{\B^{1/2}\bar{\vct}}_{L^2}\le\norm{\B^{1/2}\vct}_{L^2}+\norm{\B^{1/2}\mathbf w}_{L^2}$ and bound the fluctuation term by $\beta_BC_P^2\norm{\nabla\vct}_{L^2}^2$, $\beta_B=\norm{\B}_{L^\infty}$. Combine the two to bound the full $H^1$ norm; Lax--Milgram gives existence and uniqueness.}{% source: Ch. 3 proof of thm:unified_wellposedness; admissible lower bound, not optimal.
An admissible lower bound is $c_0=\min\{m/(2|\Omega|),\alpha^2/(1+C_P^2+2|\Omega|\beta_BC_P^2/m)\}$; it deteriorates linearly as $m$ decreases. If asked about stability (eq.\ abstract stability): with common bounds $\norm{\B_i}_{L^\infty}\le\beta_B$, $\mathbf e^\top(\int\B_i)\mathbf e\ge m$ for unit $\mathbf e$, $\norm{b_i}_{L^2}\le R_b$, the same $c_0$ gives $\norm{\vct_1^*-\vct_2^*}_{H^1}\le c_0^{-1}\norm{b_1-b_2}_{L^2}+c_0^{-2}R_b\norm{\B_1-\B_2}_{L^\infty}$. It concerns the represented coefficients; linking it to raw sensor errors or to the true motion needs a model of the representation error. If $\int\B$ is singular, a constant velocity direction is uncontrolled (discussion page on necessity of integrated directional information).}

\talknote{Problem statement for stereo; about 75 seconds.}{The third problem, the right panel of the three estimates at the start. Right: the scene, and what we want, the distance to every point; blue is near, red is far. Left: why two cameras. One camera confuses small-and-near with large-and-far. Two cameras a known distance apart, the baseline $b_{\rm cam}$, see the same point at different image positions; after rectification the shift is purely horizontal, and similar triangles turn that shift, the disparity $d$, into depth.}{So the real unknown is the match: which pixel in the right image sees the same point as each pixel in the left. Once we have it, depth is one division. How do we find matches? Frame stereo compares brightness patterns, which blur and glare corrupt. With events, a moving edge makes a pixel fire at the instant the edge crosses it. Both cameras watch the same edge, so the matching left and right pixels fire at nearly the same times.
